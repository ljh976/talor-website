Product Thesis

Talor was built around the belief that individual profiles can become personal storefronts, not only for physical goods, but for skills, services, requests, offers, negotiation, and social commerce.

Most commerce platforms start with the object being sold: a product, a shop, or a formal business. Talor starts with the person. The goal is to help individuals make their abilities discoverable, requestable, negotiable, and commercially useful.

Why I Built Talor

Many people give up things they are good at or passionate about in favor of practical and conventional choices. Someone may have wanted to become a chef but became an engineer. Someone may have loved history but became a doctor or lawyer. Others may have a useful skill or personal talent but have never had a simple way to test whether that ability has economic value.

Talor was created for those people.

The product gives individuals a place to present their abilities, price their work, and potentially earn from what they already know, enjoy, or do well.

Why Existing Platforms Are Not Enough

Large social platforms increasingly support commerce, but most of them still focus on selling products. Talor was designed around a broader idea: personal capability as commerce.

A user should be able to sell a cooking skill, creative service, technical help, handmade item, local service, or something highly personal that does not fit neatly into a standard e-commerce category.

Talor supports listings, requests, offers, negotiation, photos, social activity, and flexible transaction formats such as pickup, meetup, online delivery, and future shipping flows.

The goal is for each profile to function almost like a personal store: a place to display what someone can do, what they sell, what they are open to making, and how others can request or negotiate around that value.

Why Not Build a Viral AI Wrapper?

I considered the AI app trend, but I was skeptical of the durability of many thin AI utility products. If a user can upload the same image or prompt to a general-purpose AI assistant and get a similar result, a standalone app needs a much stronger reason to exist.

Instead of building a short-term AI wrapper, I wanted to build toward a platform that could accumulate its own users, listings, relationships, marketplace activity, and unique human-generated data.

LLMs are powerful, but they cannot fully replace data and resources that do not exist online or are not accessible to the model. A user cannot ask an LLM to buy a specific grandmother's homemade soup if that offering is not listed anywhere. A user cannot discover a neighbor's handmade accessory, a local service, or an individual's unique skill if that data has never been structured or made searchable.

AI-Assisted Development

Talor was built with heavy use of AI-assisted development workflows. I used Claude, ChatGPT, GitHub Copilot, and Copilot-style tools to accelerate planning, implementation, debugging, refactoring, UI iteration, test scenario generation, and release checklist thinking.

AI was an acceleration layer, not a replacement for engineering judgment. I retained ownership over product decisions, architecture decisions, implementation tradeoffs, review decisions, and release decisions. I manually reviewed high-risk areas such as payments, authentication boundaries, data integrity, release behavior, and user-facing workflows.

What I Learned

Building Talor taught me much more than how to ship an app.

It gave me a full 0-to-1 product experience. I had to think through the architecture of the app, connect mobile and backend systems, make infrastructure decisions, consider operating costs, and take responsibility for the product from idea to release.

The hardest lessons were not purely technical. I learned how difficult marketplace cold start is. A product can look polished and function correctly, but without users, liquidity, and trust, the product still has no real value.

I also learned that attracting sellers is not as simple as offering lower fees. Sellers already have habits, existing platforms, and a strong resistance to trying a new app. User acquisition, positioning, marketing, and image-making are not secondary details. They are part of the product.

Shipping features is only one part of building a product. The larger challenge is creating a system where users understand the value, trust the platform, and have a reason to come back.
